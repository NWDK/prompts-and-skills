# Decisions: triangulate load-bearing facts

## This replaced a heavier piece

The queued version was a research method: run a question across two
deliberately asymmetric model tracks, one with your context and one from a
different family on a clean session, then reconcile the claims by upstream
evidence lineage. 338 lines with four prompt templates.

It's a real method and I use it, but it answers a question most people
don't have. It assumes you run formal research passes across multiple
models. The thing people actually ask me about is smaller and happens
constantly: the assistant grabbed a number and now everything is built on
it.

So this is the load-bearing rule on its own, aimed at someone fairly new to
working with AI, and the two-track method is one paragraph at the bottom
for anyone who does run research passes.

Test for whether that was right: the smaller version is usable in the next
conversation you have, with no setup. The larger one needs a project.

## The trigger is the hard part, not the action

"Get a second source" is advice everyone already agrees with and nobody
applies, because applied to everything it's paralysing.

The rule only works with a threshold attached, and load-bearing is the one
that holds: does future work rest on this being right? That converts a
vague virtue into a question with a yes or no answer, at a moment you can
actually notice.

Everything else in the piece serves that. The failure mode isn't people
disagreeing with corroboration, it's people not knowing when to spend the
two minutes.

## "Tell me if you can't" is doing more work than it looks

Without it, asking an assistant for two independent sources reliably
produces two sources. Whether or not two exist.

That single clause converts the request from a retrieval task into a
question with a real negative answer available. It's the difference between
the rule working and the rule producing theatre.

## The graduated response, rather than a gate

Early drafts stopped at "corroborate it or don't use it". Wrong, and it's
the reason rules like this get abandoned: sometimes there is genuinely one
source and the work still has to happen.

So the piece says use it, mark it, keep it soft. A single-source fact can
lean a decision, never gate one. That distinction is what makes it
survivable in real work, and it's lifted directly from how I actually
handle it rather than from how it ought to work.

## The worked example is sanitised, and checked

It's real: a ratio pulled mid-trend from a dashboard that had logged three
successive readings and an explicit note that it would keep falling, then
repeated across about eight documents and into four briefs other people
acted on.

Published without the company, product, platform or figures. What survives
is the shape, which is the transferable part: not a wrong fact, but a fact
that stopped being current while everything downstream kept treating it as
though it were.

The multiplier came out on the last pass. An earlier draft said the real
figure was "different by roughly ten times", which was me rounding a
remembered number for effect in a piece about not doing that. It now says
"a small fraction", which is true and needs no precision I'd have to
defend.
