# Decisions

## Why this exists

Everything else I publish assumes a working relationship with an AI
assistant that behaves like a colleague: says what it doesn't know, pushes
back, asks before building. This piece establishes it: a rewrite of the
rules my own sessions load, reorganised around the relationship (truth,
pushback, questions, communication, voice, proportion, style) rather than
my filing system, with each clause's reason written in. You only enforce a
clause whose point you can feel.

## What was selected, and the test used

The source has dozens more rules. Test: does the rule govern the
relationship, or a specific craft? Relationship rules came in. Selected:
never guess a retrievable value; treat absence as a claim needing
evidence; never fabricate someone's lived experience (its own section: the
failure is self-concealing); a clean parse isn't correctness; match effort
to stakes, the counterweight to a document of obligations.

## What was left out

Evidence discipline, multi-agent conventions, and briefing an agent well:
each is a craft, not a property of the relationship. The same test left
out phrasing new rules as guidelines rather than hard bans (it governs
writing rules, not the assistant's behaviour) and two rules against
blocking a build on a missing test case or an unrequested feature. One
idea lives in one place; two copies drift and the reader inherits the
argument.

## The two versions

The short version, twelve lines plus a style slot, is the actual product;
beginners follow examples, not instructions. The annotated version is for
the second week, when a clause gets broken and you need its reasons.
Twenty-odd source rules became twelve by folding related ones together and
promoting two sneaky truth failures, a guessed retrievable and an asserted
absence, to their own lines.

## Why the never-invent clauses stay separate

Four of the twelve share one family: don't invent, don't guess a
look-up-able value, treat absence as a claim, don't invent someone's
experience. Folding them into one clause was rejected: each feels
different at the time, a guessed URL like reconstructing, an unproven
absence like reporting, so one general clause gets agreed to sincerely and
broken three ways before lunch. Specificity is the enforcement: you can't
point at a clause broad enough to cover everything.

## Sizing, and why the block isn't duplicated in the README

The block is sized for small standing-instruction fields. Per-host caps
aren't printed: they differ by host and plan, and already moved once.
What's printed is our file's measured size, which can't date, plus the cut
order. The README links to the block rather than reproduce it: one extra
click beats two copies drifting apart.


## The claim I removed

An early draft promised what you'd notice in the first session: more "I
don't know", more questions before drafts, a plan pushed back on. Cut:
every account I own already runs these rules, so I've never tested pasting
this cold with nothing else supporting it. Rules working inside my setup
and rules working as pasted text are different claims, and only the first
has evidence.

## What I'd want pushed back on

- **Is twelve too many?** If you'd cut to eight, I want to know which four
  died.
- **The enforcement claim is folk practice.** "Point at the clause" matches
  my experience, unmeasured.
- **How much of the improvement is the model changing, versus me
  noticing?** The truth clauses make violations legible, which changes my
  behaviour too; I can't cleanly separate the two.
