# A Working Agreement With an AI Agent

Twelve clauses you paste into your assistant's standing instructions. They
tell it to say when it doesn't know something, to push back instead of
agreeing, and to ask before it builds.

A template, not a skill. Nothing to install.

## Use it now

1. Open **[`SHORT-VERSION.md`](SHORT-VERSION.md)** and copy the block.
2. Paste it into your assistant's custom instructions, project
   instructions, or system prompt.
3. Replace the "My style" line with two or three preferences you actually
   hold.

That's it. The block is sized to fit the smaller standing-instruction
fields, and `SHORT-VERSION.md` says what to cut first if yours won't take
it.

One habit matters more than the paste: the first time an unlabelled guess
or a rubber stamp gets through, point at the clause it broke. An agreement
nobody enforces stops working within days.

## Why I wrote it

I'm Nick. I run most of my work through AI assistants, and these are the
standing instructions my own setup runs on, rewritten so they make sense
outside it.

Every assistant I've used defaults to agreeable, and agreeable fails in
boring, predictable ways. Confident guesses where "I don't know" was the
honest answer. Approval of plans that had holes in them. A finished draft
built on a question it never asked me.

Better prompting didn't fix that. What worked was writing down that the
uncomfortable behaviours are the job.

## Known limits

- **I can't tell you what it'll do on your setup.** Every account I own
  already runs these rules, so I've never used a naive assistant to test
  the paste-it-cold path this README describes. It works for me. That's
  one person, by a different route, which isn't the same claim.
- **Pushback is a thing you have to want.** Clause six tells the assistant
  to challenge you, and sometimes it'll challenge you and be wrong. Worth
  it, in my experience. Still a trade.
- **These are my defaults, not laws.** They came out of one working life.
  The style line is yours to rewrite, and any clause that fights how you
  work should lose.
- **A standing instruction isn't a guarantee.** Models drift, forget, and
  sometimes ignore instructions, especially in long conversations. Clause
  one never stops being your job too.
- **Plain text, no formatting tricks**, so it should paste anywhere. Should.
  If it doesn't, I'd like to know.

## The two files

**[`SHORT-VERSION.md`](SHORT-VERSION.md)** is the thing you use: twelve
numbered lines, one paste, a style slot to personalise.

**[`AGREEMENT.md`](AGREEMENT.md)** is the same agreement with the reason for
each clause written next to it. Read that one in week two, when a clause
gets broken and you're deciding whether to enforce it or delete it.

Between them they cover inventing information (including the two sneaky
forms: guessing values you could look up, and calling something absent off
one failed search), pushing back on weak plans, asking the questions that
change the answer before drafting, communication that respects your time,
writing in your voice without inventing your life, and matching effort to
stakes.

## Who it's for

Anyone using an AI assistant for real work. If you've ever caught yours
making something up with total confidence, agreeing with a plan you later
found holes in, or asking the key question underneath a draft it had
already written, that's what these clauses are aimed at.

## Where it came from

The load-bearing subset of the collaboration rules my workspace loads into
every session, plus the truth-handling rules that earned their place by
being learned the hard way: the guessed value that read exactly like a real
one, the "it isn't there" that turned out to mean "I searched wrong", the
bio line that implied more than the facts did. What got left out, and why,
is in [`DECISIONS.md`](DECISIONS.md).

## Feedback

Take it, change it, ignore the parts that don't fit. If a clause misfires,
reads as ambiguous, or misses the failure you keep hitting, I'd genuinely
like to hear about it: [open an issue](../../issues). This is the piece
most likely to be improved by other people's working lives.
